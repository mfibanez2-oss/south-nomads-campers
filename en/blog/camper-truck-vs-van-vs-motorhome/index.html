Camper truck, campervan or motorhome: what actually works in Patagonia

There's no perfect vehicle, only the right vehicle for your route. This is the comparison I give when someone asks whether to rent a pickup camper, a van or a motorhome for southern Chile.

When people write for advice they rarely ask about the vehicle — they ask about the price. And yet the decision that shapes the trip most is what you're driving when the gravel turnoff appears with a hand-painted wooden sign.

The three formats, plainly

Camper truck (a camper on a pickup)

A living module mounted on the bed of a 4x4 pickup. It's what we rent. Advantages: high clearance, real traction, mechanics any small-town garage understands, and if the module has an issue the vehicle is still a vehicle.

Campervan

A van converted inside. Lighter, cheaper, more discreet, easier to park. Almost always 2WD, with low ground clearance and suspension designed for urban loads rather than washboard.

Motorhome

The full coachbuilt RV. Fixed bathroom with shower, more square metres, more water autonomy. In Chile it's a great option for the central regions and Route 5, and an uncomfortable choice for the Carretera Austral, where roads are narrow, curves are blind, and trucks are frequent.

Gravel and back roads: the real difference

Southern Chile has three kinds of road, and it's worth being clear about them:

  • Pavement: all three formats are fine. No argument here.
  • Consolidated gravel: most of the Carretera Austral. A van gets through slowly, punished by washboard; a camper truck gets through on suspension built for it.
  • Loose gravel, mud, fords and steep climbs: the good detours. This is where the van stops or suffers, and where the motorhome simply doesn't go.

The point isn't off-road heroics, which you don't need. The point is not having to decide. When the vehicle can, you take the turnoff. When it can't, you talk yourself into believing it probably wasn't that special.

Comfort, space and life on board

To be fair: on square metres the motorhome always wins, and a well-designed van is surprisingly efficient. What decides it in practice is something else:

  • Heating. In Patagonia this matters more than size. A diesel heater independent of the vehicle's tank changes the whole trip, and not every vehicle has one.
  • A fixed bed versus a bed you build. Making up the bed each night seems minor until night ten, in the rain, at eleven o'clock.
  • Standing height. Being able to stand up to cook is the difference between cooking and eating bread.
  • Electrical autonomy. Solar plus an auxiliary battery is what lets you spend three nights without a hookup, which is exactly what you want inside a national park.

Fuel, ferries and parking

Vans use less fuel, true. But there are costs that don't appear on the spec sheet:

  • Parking in towns: the van clearly wins. A camper truck fits a normal parking space; a motorhome often doesn't.
  • Ferries: fares are charged by vehicle length. A long motorhome pays noticeably more on every leg.
  • Repairs: a common Japanese or German pickup gets fixed in any southern town. An imported motorhome does not.
  • Wind: more side area means more work at the wheel in Patagonia. You feel that by the end of the day.

Which one suits your trip

  • Carretera Austral, Aysén, national parks, back-road Chiloé: 4x4 camper truck. That's not marketing, it's geography.
  • Lake district with occasional hotels and main roads: a van does the job and saves you money.
  • Route 5 south from Santiago with city stops: motorhome or van.
  • Traveling with small kids and a lot of rain: interior space wins; between a large camper truck and a motorhome, decide by how far off the pavement you'll go.

Why we rent camper trucks only

We could run vans. They'd be cheaper to buy, cheaper to operate, and they'd rent out just as well. We don't have them for one concrete reason: half the complaints in a mixed fleet come from people who got as far as the vehicle could go rather than as far as they wanted to go.

We build our campers ourselves in Puerto Varas, on 4x4 pickups, with the suspension, all-terrain tyres and heater chosen for the same roads we drive every season. When something breaks we know which part it is, because we fitted it.

Frequently asked

What's the difference between a camper and a motorhome?

A camper is a living module mounted on a pickup: two parts, pickup mechanics, high clearance. A motorhome is a single coachbuilt vehicle, bigger and more comfortable inside but much more limited on narrow roads and gravel.

Do you need 4x4 in southern Chile?

On the main route in summer, not always. For the detours that make the trip worthwhile — viewpoints, beaches, park access roads, anything muddy after rain — 4x4 stops being a luxury and becomes the thing that gets you there.

Is a camper truck more expensive than a campervan?

Per day, usually yes, and it uses a bit more fuel. The fair comparison includes where each one can actually take you: if your route forces you to turn around at three detours, the van was expensive.

How many people fit in a camper truck?

In our larger one, the Nomads L, up to four. The Nomads M is built for two, with more usable space each. Four adults in any camper this size is tight on a long trip.
