Where to sleep in a camper in Chile: campsites, wild camping and the real rules

After price, this is the question I get most: "can I sleep anywhere?" The short answer is no. The long answer is better, because Chile has so many good options that wild camping stops being necessary.

There's a fantasy that sells well on social media: the camper parked alone in front of a lake, nobody around, anywhere you please. It exists, and I've lived it several times. But it doesn't work the way it looks, and believing Chile is a 4,000-kilometre free car park is the fastest route to a conversation with a police officer, a landowner or a park ranger.

What the rules say, and what happens in practice

Start with what's clear:

  • Private land without permission: no. And a lot of southern Chile that looks like "empty countryside" has an owner. If there's a fence, there's an owner.
  • National parks: designated areas only. CONAF defines the camping zones; outside them you don't camp and you don't overnight.
  • Beaches and lake shores: public access exists, but the road that reaches them is often private. Access is one thing; parking overnight is another.
  • Towns: some municipalities expressly prohibit sleeping in vehicles on the waterfront or in squares. The signs are there, even if they're hard to spot at night.

Now the practice: on rural southern roads, sleeping inside the vehicle — no table out, no awning, no fire, arriving late and leaving early — is something almost nobody will challenge. The gap between that and "camping" is enormous, legally and socially.

The rule I use

If I have to ask myself whether I'm allowed to be here, I find somewhere else. The eleven o'clock conversation with a torch tapping the window is never worth it.

The four kinds of place you'll sleep

1. Private campsites

The most common option and the most underrated. In the south they cost little, and they have water, toilets, often a hot shower and power — and they're usually run by a family who lives there and will tell you where to go tomorrow. In a camper you don't need the facilities, but a hot shower every third day changes the mood of a trip.

2. CONAF camping areas

Inside the national parks. Basic, in unbeatable surroundings, with marked pitches and sometimes shelters and dry toilets. Charged per person, and full in high season. They're the only legal way to sleep inside a park.

3. Municipal and designated areas

Several Carretera Austral villages have areas where the municipality allows camping vehicles to park. They aren't always signposted, so asking at the municipal office, the fuel station or the village shop works better than any app.

4. Overnighting with judgement (wild camping)

Parking overnight somewhere public and discreet without setting up. It works well when you follow the house rules: arrive late, leave early, nothing outside, no fire, no noise, zero rubbish.

How to choose a spot before dark

My first rule of Patagonia: by 17:00 I know where I'm sleeping. Searching after dark, on gravel, in the rain, is how bad decisions get made. What I check:

  • Level ground. Sleeping on a slope ruins the night, and the fridge works harder.
  • Wind shelter. A bank, a stand of trees or a building transforms the night. If there's no shelter, park nose into the wind.
  • Firm ground. Sand and mud look harmless at nine at night and are not at seven in the morning.
  • No big branches overhead. Patagonian wind plus a dead branch is an expensive combination.
  • Out of watercourses. Never in a dry riverbed or on a low gravel bank. Water rises at night.
  • Blocking nothing. Not gates, not service tracks, not anyone's way home.

The apps and maps I use

  • iOverlander: the best one for vehicle travelers in South America. Spots verified by other people, with notes on access and safety. Download the region before you lose signal.
  • Park4Night: more popular with European travelers and very useful in the lake district.
  • Maps.me or offline Google Maps: essential. You'll spend hours without data on the Carretera Austral.
  • Windy: for the wind, which is the variable that actually decides where you park.

A caveat on apps: spots reviewed three years ago may be closed, fenced or now a paid campsite. Always keep a plan B within 30 minutes.

Where to sleep, region by region

Around Puerto Varas and Lake Llanquihue

Good campsites in Ensenada toward Vicente Pérez Rosales National Park, and along the lake shore toward Frutillar. It's the easiest part of the trip: infrastructure, signal and a supermarket nearby. A good first night while you get used to the camper.

Cochamó and the Reloncaví estuary

Small family campsites, narrow gravel roads, and one of the most beautiful landscapes within two hours of Puerto Varas.

Chiloé

Seafront campsites at Cucao and along the west coast, plus Chiloé National Park. Watch the wind and the tide — always ask how far the water comes up.

Northern Carretera Austral (Chaitén to Coyhaique)

Pumalín has excellent camping areas with marked pitches and trailheads. La Junta, Puyuhuapi and Villa Amengual have simple, cheap campsites.

Southern Aysén (Río Tranquilo to Villa O'Higgins)

Less supply, more demand in January. Puerto Río Tranquilo fills up; Cochrane is a better base than it looks; Caleta Tortel makes you leave the vehicle in the entrance car park, so plan that night before you arrive.

Camper etiquette: don't close the spot for the next person

This isn't moralising, it's self-interest. Every spot that gets closed in Patagonia gets closed because somebody left rubbish, lit a fire, or set up for three days with an awning at a public viewpoint.

  • Take all your rubbish, organic included. Peel doesn't break down quickly in a cold climate and it attracts animals.
  • Grey water: never into a river, lake or spring. Use campsite disposal points.
  • If there's no toilet: far from water, deep hole, and the paper comes with you.
  • No fires outside built fire structures.
  • Keep noise down after ten. In a valley, music carries for kilometres.
  • If a neighbour lets you stay on their land, thank them properly. That goodwill is what keeps this way of traveling possible.

Water, toilets, showers and rubbish

  • Drinking water: campsites, service stations, municipal taps and many village shops. Fill the tank whenever you can.
  • Showers: campsites, hot springs and municipal gyms. Our portable hot-water shower is an add-on if you want full independence.
  • Toilets: our campers don't carry a fixed chemical toilet; in practice you use campsites, service stations and designated areas, which on this route are more frequent than people expect.
  • Rubbish: town containers. Never leave bags beside a full container — dogs open them within ten minutes.

With that settled, what's left is the weather. When you go changes which campsites are open and how much wind you'll get: that's in the guide to the best time to travel Patagonia by camper.

Frequently asked

Is wild camping legal in Chile?

There's no blanket right to wild camp. Camping on private land without permission is prohibited, and so is camping inside national parks outside designated areas. In many rural areas sleeping inside your vehicle is tolerated if you leave no trace, light no fire and block nothing — but that's tolerance, not a right.

Can I sleep in the camper inside a national park?

Only in the camping areas designated by CONAF or its concessionaire, which are paid and sometimes need booking. Outside those areas it isn't allowed, and in high season rangers do check.

Can I light a fire?

Only in the built fire structures of authorised campsites. Everywhere else it's prohibited, and in Patagonia this is taken very seriously: the region's biggest fires started as campfires.

Do I need to book campsites on the Carretera Austral?

In January and February it's worth it, especially in villages with few campsites like Puerto Río Tranquilo or Villa O'Higgins. Outside those months it's rarely necessary.
